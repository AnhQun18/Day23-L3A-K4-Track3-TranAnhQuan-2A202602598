# Video and Multimodal Generation: Foundations, Architectures, Unified Models, and Evaluation

## TL;DR

- Video generation has moved through three dominant paradigms — GAN-based extension of image generators, diffusion-based systems, and autoregressive (AR) models — and diffusion became the field's backbone before AR and multimodal hybrids re-emerged [1][2].
- A single architectural recipe now dominates generative vision: compress data into a latent space, patchify it, and denoise or predict patches with a transformer, an idea first demonstrated for images [3][4] and extended to videos through spacetime latent patches [5][6].
- Diffusion objectives are increasingly paired with (or replaced by) straight-path flow/rectified-flow regression, which is reported to enable faster training, faster generation, and better performance [7][8].
- Unified multimodal models that mix next-token prediction with diffusion in one transformer — Transfusion, Chameleon, Emu3, Show-o, Janus — have become an active research frontier [9][10][11][12][13].
- Evaluation is the field's weakest link: the widely used FVD metric rests on assumptions that have been shown to fail [14], and standard benchmarks have been criticized for under-covering camera motion and video-level ranking [15][16].

## Background

**Definition.** Generative video modeling formalizes the task as modeling the conditional distribution p(y|x), where x is a conditioning signal (typically text) and y a video [1]. A video is a high-dimensional signal with two coupled structures — spatial content within a frame and temporal evolution across frames — and modern systems commonly decompose the problem into (i) a compression stage that maps pixels to a compact latent space, and (ii) a generative stage that models the latent distribution. Multimodal generation generalizes this: given sequences over text, images, audio and video, a model produces a sequence in one or more modalities, either by generating tokens or by denoising continuous latents [9][11][17].

**Why it matters.** Two motivations recur in the retrieved literature. First, "world simulation": models trained jointly on images and videos of variable duration, resolution and aspect ratio are presented as learning general-purpose visual representations of physical scenes [5]. Second, accessibility and cost: open efforts explicitly target "democratizing efficient video production for all" [6], and one 2025 report claims a commercial-level video model trained for only $200k [18]. A third motivation is unification: a single model that both understands and generates content avoids the parameter duplication of separate vision and generation stacks [12][13].

**Foundational citations.** The diffusion backbone derives from denoising diffusion probabilistic models, which produce high-quality image synthesis and "naturally admit a progressive lossy decompression scheme that can be interpreted as a generalization of autoregressive decoding" [19]. Latent diffusion moved this process into the compressed latent space of a pretrained autoencoder to cut the "hundreds of GPU days" of pixel-space optimization, and introduced cross-attention conditioning for text and bounding boxes [3]. The video extension followed: a video diffusion model described as "a natural extension of the standard image diffusion architecture" that jointly trains on images and videos and adds conditional spatial/temporal extension for longer, higher-resolution outputs [20], and a cascaded text-conditional system combining a base video model with interleaved spatial and temporal super-resolution stages [21]. In parallel, VideoGPT established the autoregressive alternative, training a VQ-VAE with 3D convolutions and axial self-attention and then modeling discrete latents with a GPT-like transformer [22].

## Latent compression, patching, and the diffusion-transformer lineage

The most influential architectural shift of the last four years is the replacement of the U-Net with a transformer operating on latent patches. The DiT paper reports that "DiTs with higher Gflops ... consistently have lower FID" and that its adaLN-Zero conditioning block yields lower FID than both cross-attention and in-context conditioning while being the most compute-efficient, with a state-of-the-art FID of 2.27 on class-conditional ImageNet 256×256 [4]. Sora's report adopts exactly this recipe for video: videos are first "compressed into a lower-dimensional latent space, and subsequently decomp[osed] ... into spacetime patches", and the authors state plainly that "Sora is a diffusion transformer", with sample quality improving "markedly as training compute increases" [5]. A third-party review of that report reverses-engineers the same pipeline as a time-space compressor, a ViT over tokenized latents, and a CLIP-like conditioning mechanism [23].

Video-specific work mostly differs in *how* attention is organized over the space-time token grid. Latte extracts spatio-temporal tokens and offers four variants that decompose the spatial and temporal axes to control token count [24]. W.A.L.T uses a causal encoder to jointly compress images and videos into a unified latent space plus window attention that alternates "between non-overlapping, window-restricted spatial and spatiotemporal attention", claiming competitive quality at up to 2× greater speed and lower memory [25]. Open-Sora's STDiT "decouples spatial and temporal attention" — spatial attention within each frame, temporal attention across frames at the same location — and reports that adding temporal attention nearly doubles parameters from 580M to 1.1B [6]. Open-Sora Plan goes the other way, arguing that factorized 2+1D attention "theoretically and practically limit[s]" performance compared to "Full 3D Attention", and controls the resulting quadratic cost with a sparse attention scheme whose sequence-length reduction scales as 1/k [26]. Trainable sparsity is also applied at training time, not just inference: VSA replaces full attention at both training and inference using a coarse tile-level stage followed by token-level attention inside selected tiles [27].

Two smaller components matter disproportionately. First, the autoencoder: compression ratios of 4×8×8 (temporal × height × width) are reported for wavelet-flow designs [26], and Open-Sora 1.2 "achiev[es] 4x compression in the temporal dimension" [6]. Second, patch conditioning: Open-Sora replaces sinusoidal positional encoding with rotary embeddings for temporal attention and applies QK-normalization for stability [6]; Qwen2-VL's M-RoPE extends rotary position encoding to disambiguate text, image and video tokens [28]; and Open-Sora Plan employs 3D RoPE inside self-attention with adaLN-Zero modulation [26].

Landmark systems instantiate these choices at scale: CogVideoX pairs a 3D VAE with an "expert transformer" [29], HunyuanVideo is described as an open-source video foundation model with over 13 billion parameters [30], and Movie Gen is presented as a suite of media foundation models that "generate high-quality videos with synchronized audio" [31]. Notably, ambition has grown from 15-second, 720p clips in open models [6] to a claimed minute of high-fidelity video in a closed one [5].

## Training objectives: from denoising diffusion to rectified flow and scaling laws

Denoising remains the base objective [19][20], but the field has substantially moved toward flow-based formulations. Flow Matching is a "simulation-free approach for training CNFs based on regressing vector fields of fixed conditional probability paths" that "subsumes existing diffusion paths as specific instances"; crucially, conditional OT paths "form straight line trajectories whereas diffusion paths result in curved paths", yielding "faster training, faster generation, and better performance" [7]. Rectified flow makes the same idea operational by learning an ODE that follows straight paths, and reports that "with just one step of reflow, the flow becomes nearly straight", achieving a one-step FID of 4.85 on CIFAR10 [8]. The combination then scaled: SD3 pairs rectified flow with a scaling study of transformer backbones up to 8B parameters, and its MM-DiT architecture "incorporates learnable streams for both image and text tokens" with separate weights per modality — an explicit critique of feeding "a fixed text representation ... directly into the model" via cross-attention [32].

Scaling behavior is now studied directly for video. One study reports that scaling laws for video diffusion transformers do exist, but that "unlike language models, video diffusion models are more sensitive to learning rate and batch size", and it claims 40.1% lower inference cost within a 10¹⁰ TFLOP compute budget by choosing sizes with the fitted law [33]. Adjacent image-domain work finds that compute-optimal text-to-image training "requir[es] far more data per parameter than language models" with robust overtraining behavior and universal curve shapes [34]. Scaling is also being redirected toward inference: Video-T1 reframes test-time scaling as "a searching problem to sample better trajectories from Gaussian noise space", using a Tree-of-Frames procedure, and reports that "increasing test-time compute consistently leads to significant improvements" in video quality [35].

## Autoregressive and hybrid paradigms

Autoregressive video generation has returned as a serious alternative. VideoGPT's two-stage VQ-VAE-plus-GPT design already reached an FVD of 103 on the BAIR Robot dataset [22], and newer work relaxes its assumptions. NOVA casts video generation as "non-quantized autoregressive modeling of temporal frame-by-frame prediction and spatial set-by-set prediction", motivated partly by the tension in token-based methods between "high fidelity and high compression simultaneously" and by diffusion models that "only learn the joint distribution of fixed-length frames"; it reports a VBench score of 80.1 at 0.6B parameters with 2.75 FPS after 342 GPU days on A100-40G [36]. Progressive noise assignment is proposed to generate long videos "without quality degradation or abrupt scene changes" [37]. Others integrate attention-level changes: Next-Frame Diffusion combines block-wise causal attention, consistency distillation and speculative sampling for real-time generation [38], VideoAR combines multi-scale next-frame prediction with autoregressive modeling [39], and Mask Forcing addresses mode collapse in distilled AR video diffusion by injecting masked cleaner signals during self-rollout [40]. Streaming world models push autoregression toward continuous, interactive generation and navigation rather than fixed-length clips [41].

The deeper trend is hybridization. Transfusion trains "a single transformer over mixed-modality sequences" by combining next-token prediction loss with diffusion, pretraining models up to 7B parameters and reporting that it "scales significantly better than quantizing images and training a language model over discrete image tokens" [9]. Chameleon instead stays token-based via early fusion, "capable of understanding and generating images and text in any arbitrary sequence" [10], while Emu3 pushes the pure-token line further by tokenizing "images, text, and videos into a discrete space" and training a single transformer solely with next-token prediction [11]. Show-o unifies "autoregressive and (discrete) diffusion modeling" in one transformer across VQA, text-to-image, inpainting and mixed-modality generation [12]. Janus identifies a key failure mode of single-encoder designs — Chameleon-style shared encoders are "suboptimal" because understanding and generation need different granularities — and decouples visual encoding while keeping a unified transformer [13]. At the frontier, Omni-Diffusion claims to be "the first any-to-any multimodal language model built entirely on mask-based discrete diffusion models" spanning text, speech and images, arguing that diffusion "support[s] parallel decoding" and steerable generation trajectories [17]. ILLUME+ shows the tokenizer is still the bottleneck, noting that VQGAN-based discretization in Chameleon/Emu3 "lag[s] behind specialist models like LLaVA" on understanding, and adds a diffusion decoder for fidelity [42]. A 2026 controlled study re-uses Transfusion's recipe — "next-token prediction for language and diffusion for vision" — over text, image-text pairs, video, and action-conditioned video, evidence that the hybrid recipe is still an active pretraining paradigm [43].

## Controllability and spatial conditioning

Controllability research splits into conditioning *mechanisms* and conditioning *signals*. The mechanism lineage runs from LDM's cross-attention for text and bounding boxes and concatenation of spatially aligned inputs for image-to-image tasks [3], to ControlNet, which "locks the production-ready large diffusion models" and learns controls through zero-initialized convolutions for edges, depth, segmentation and human pose, with training reported as robust across datasets smaller than 50k and larger than 1M images [44]. SD3's MM-DiT replaces fixed cross-attention conditioning with bidirectional image–text streams [32], and DiT found adaLN-Zero more compute-efficient than cross-attention or in-context conditioning [4].

For video, the distinctive new signal is trajectory. ATI is described as a unified framework integrating camera movement, object translation and local motion through trajectory-based inputs [45], and CT-1 learns camera trajectories through diffusion transformers with a wavelet regularization loss to produce camera-controllable video [46]. Both rest on Hugging Face listing-level summaries rather than full primary text, so these descriptions should be treated as provisional. More broadly, the field's control vocabulary — text, image, depth, pose, camera path — is now shared across image and video models rather than being modality-specific [44][45][46].

## Efficiency, streaming, and cost

Because attention over space-time tokens is quadratic, efficiency is a first-class research area. Two strategies dominate the retrieved evidence. The first is distillation into few-step, causal generators: DMD is extended to videos to distill a 50-step diffusion model into a 4-step generator that "supports fast streaming generation of high quality videos at 9.4 FPS on a single GPU" via KV caching [47]; Magic 1-For-1 factorizes text-to-video into text-to-image plus image-to-video, generating "5-second video clips within 3 seconds" and a minute-long clip within a minute using adversarial step distillation, prior-condition injection and parameter sparsification [48]. Self Forcing reports "real-time video generation at 17 FPS with sub-second latency on a single H100 GPU" using a rolling KV cache and stochastic gradient truncation, while matching or surpassing slower non-causal models [49]. The second strategy is sparse or restructured attention: MC-Sparse is a training-free scheme selecting individual KV tokens and grouping similar queries into tile-aligned groups, reporting a 1.80× denoising speedup on Minimax-H3-Base at negligible quality loss [50], complementing the trainable sparsity of VSA [27] and Skiparse [26].

Infrastructure-level work targets memory and precision: one 2026 system reports an NVFP4-based parallel infrastructure "throughout the full training and inference workflow of long video generation, addressing speed and memory bottlenecks" [51]. Cost transparency is nonetheless uneven. Open-Sora 2.0 states its training cost is "5-10 times lower than comparable models like MovieGen and Step-Video-T2V" while performing "comparably to leading video generation models including HunyuanVideo and Runway Gen-3 Alpha" [18]. Yet no retrieved source supplied energy or carbon figures; targeted searches for energy/carbon costs returned no results, so efficiency claims in this literature should be read as latency and memory claims, not sustainability claims.

## Benchmarking multimodal and video generation

Quality assessment has professionalized in two directions: suites that decompose quality into named dimensions, and metrics that try to be composite.

VBench targets the fact that "existing metrics do not fully align with human perceptions" by defining hierarchical, disentangled dimensions with tailored prompts [52]. Its public leaderboard enumerates 16 text-to-video dimensions (subject and background consistency, temporal flickering, motion smoothness, dynamic degree, aesthetic and imaging quality, object class, multiple objects, human action, color, spatial relationship, scene, temporal style, appearance style, overall consistency), aggregates them into Quality and Semantic scores with per-dimension min–max normalization, and reported 28 T2V and 12 I2V models as of its 08/2024 update, and VBench-2.0 introduces "a structured evaluation suite comprising five broad categories and 18 fine-grained capability dimensions", including "commonsense reasoning, physics-based realism, human motion, and creative composition" [53]. VBench++ extends this "to support a wide range of video generation tasks ... including text-to-video and image-to-video, with an adaptive Image Suite", and adds trustworthiness evaluation [54]. EvalCrafter argues that judging large conditional models by "a few metrics, e.g., FVD or IS" is inadequate and builds an exhaustive multi-aspect pipeline claimed to align with user opinions [55].

Compositional and object-centric evaluation developed in parallel for images. GenEval observes that "metrics like FID or CLIPScore only offer a holistic measure ... unsuited for fine-grained or instance-level analysis", scores co-occurrence, position, count and color with object detectors, and reports 83% agreement with annotators versus 88% between annotators, higher than CLIPScore on the more complex tasks, while noting models still lack "complex capabilities such as spatial relations and attribute binding" [56]. T2I-CompBench++ provides "8,000 compositional text prompts categorized into four primary groups: attribute binding, object relationships, generative numeracy, and complex compositions", with a separate metric designed for each compositional sub-task because no single metric covers them all [57].

Two more recent directions are worth flagging. Human-preference arenas: K-Sort Arena argues that traditional vote-based arenas "require an excessive number of comparisons for ranking to converge and are vulnerable to preference noise", exploiting k-wise comparisons for faster convergence [58]. And safety: T2VSafetyBench defines 12 critical aspects of video generation safety, builds prompts from real, LLM-generated and jailbreak sources, and reports that "no single model excels in all aspects" and that there is "a trade-off between the usability and safety" of text-to-video models [59].

## Trends and open problems

**Metrics are being rebuilt.** Beyond FVD reports three specific failures of the field's default metric: "the non-Gaussianity of the Inflated 3D Convnet (I3D) feature space", "the insensitivity of I3D features to temporal distortions", and the "impractical sample sizes required for reliable estimation", concluding that FVD "falls short as a standalone metric" [14]. This corroborates earlier critiques [55] and motivates newer composite metrics such as the World Consistency Score, which combines object permanence, relation stability, causal compliance and a flicker penalty via a learned weighted formula [60].

**Benchmarks under-cover motion and long-horizon narrative.** DynamicEval argues that VBench and EvalCrafter emphasize "subject-centric prompts or static camera scenes" while "camera motion essential for producing cinematic shots ... under dynamic motion are largely unexplored", and that aggregating video-level scores into one model-level score "overlook[s] video-level evaluation" needed to pick the better of two candidate videos [15]. NarrLV similarly notes that long-video assessment "primarily relies on benchmarks with simple narrative prompts (e.g., VBench)" even though the goal is richer narrative content over longer durations [16].

**Physics and world consistency remain unsolved.** VideoPhy-2 finds that for real-world actions such as playing tennis or a backflip "their adherence to physical commonsense ... remains unclear", and criticizes existing benchmarks for "limited size, lack of human evaluation, sim-to-real gaps, and absence of fine-grained physical rule analysis" [61]. Newer world-model work folds this into model design: a multi-agent framework targets "accurate multi-agent control while maintaining multi-view consistency" [62]; a robot world model aims at "action-faithful and physically plausible video prediction" while noting that imprecise calibration impairs action following [63].

**Disputed framing: quality versus reproducibility.** Sora's report explicitly states that "model and implementation details are not included in this report" [5], and a review notes the report "merely presents a high-level idea, making reproduction challenging for the research community" [23]. Claims about that system's internals therefore rest on third-party inference rather than author disclosure — a recurring pattern in commercial reports, which describe capabilities while withholding implementation detail [30][18].

**Open technical problems.** Four recur across sources. (i) Long-video coherence and temporal consistency, addressed through progressive noise assignment [37], masked rollouts [40], narrative-centric benchmarks [16], and streaming world models that generate long continuous video [41]. (ii) The tokenizer bottleneck: discrete latents must simultaneously support high fidelity and high compression [36], and VQ-based discretization lags on understanding [42]. (iii) Cost — quadratic 3D attention [26][27], and GPU memory in long sequences [50] — with dollar figures asserted but rarely audited [18]. (iv) Misuse and safety: deepfake research documents non-consensual explicit videos and synthetic recreations of deceased public figures without family consent, alongside regulatory pressure for labeling, watermarking and traceability [64], while safety benchmarks show no model dominating all risk aspects [59]. A related unresolved gap is evaluation of novel-view and embodied generation, where methods that lift video into explicit 3D representations are criticized for translating "depth errors into misplaced content" [62].

**Where the field is heading.** The most recent surveys frame a transition "from early GANs to dominant diffusion models, and further to emerging AR-based and multimodal techniques" [1], naming both closed systems and open contenders such as Wan and HunyuanVideo in the same landscape [1][30]. If that trajectory holds, the interesting questions are no longer whether transformers or diffusion win, but how the two are combined — shared latent spaces [25], shared transformers with modality-specific weights [32], or shared objectives across next-token prediction and denoising [9][43] — and how those combinations can be evaluated in a way that survives criticism of FVD and static-prompt benchmarks [14][15].
