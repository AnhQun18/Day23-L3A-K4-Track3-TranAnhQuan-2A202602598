# Efficient Inference and Small Language Models: A Survey of Compression, Architecture, Training and Deployment

## TL;DR

- The field has converged on a layered efficiency stack rather than a single trick: quantization and pruning shrink weights, attention and KV-cache redesigns shrink memory state, speculative decoding and paged serving raise hardware utilization, and curated-data training makes small models competitive at their own scale [1][2][3][4][5].
- Quantization reports roughly 1.5-4.5x speedups and ~4x memory reductions with "negligible" accuracy loss, but the wins depend on hardware support for mixed-precision operands; GPTQ's authors note their method gave no speedup for the multiplications themselves "due to the lack of hardware support for mixed-precision operands" [2][6][7].
- Small language models are trained by deliberate overtraining: TinyLlama uses ~1.1B parameters on up to 3T tokens, Phi-3-mini uses 3.8B parameters on 3.3T heavily filtered and synthetic tokens, SmolLM2 uses 1.7B parameters on ~11T tokens, and Gemma 2 trains its 2B and 9B models with knowledge distillation instead of pure next-token prediction [8][5][9][10].
- KV-cache and state memory, not parameter count, is now the binding constraint for long-context inference; reported results include up to 400x KV compression, an 8x smaller KV cache in a hybrid Mamba-Transformer design, and more than 10x throughput from KV eviction in a sparse-first engine [11][12][13].
- Energy and on-device throughput are measured rather than asserted: a controlled study found a CPU-only llama.cpp configuration on an iPhone 15 Pro reaching 17 tokens/second versus 12.8 with GPU acceleration, and an energy benchmark reports optimizations that "sometimes more than 40%" cut energy without changing the computation [14][15].

## Background

"Efficient inference" here means reducing the time, memory, energy, or monetary cost of running a language model for generation, without a proportional loss in output quality. "Small language models" (SLMs) are models small enough to be constrained in deployment; one survey defines them as models aiming to "retain the accuracy and/or adaptability of large language models, while being subject to some constraint(s), such as training or inference hardware, data availability, bandwidth, or generation time," and notes SLMs are "ideal for various settings including on-device, mobile, edge devices" [16]. The same survey warns the boundary is contextual: GPT-2 at 1.5B parameters is smaller than many models now called small [16]. SmolLM2's authors place the practical ceiling near 3 billion parameters or less, describing such models as "computationally inexpensive and can be run on a wider range of devices (e.g. mobile phones)" [9].

The two topics are coupled by economics: because inference cost scales with the parameters that must be stored and moved, a compression survey frames the area as reducing the "significant computational and storage resources" large models demand [1]. Compute-optimal scaling supplies the theoretical bridge, showing that "for compute-optimal training, the model size and the number of training tokens should be scaled equally," and that for the Gopher compute budget an optimal model should be about four times smaller while trained on four times more tokens, which "reduces inference cost considerably" [17]. That insight — a smaller model trained longer is cheaper to serve and, at fixed compute, more capable — underlies every small-model recipe below.

The toolkit itself has a documented taxonomy: compression splits into "quantization, pruning, knowledge distillation, and low-rank factorization," with quantization divided into weight-only, weight-activation, and KV-cache variants, pruning into unstructured, semi-structured, and structured variants, and distillation into black-box and white-box settings [1]. Foundational work predates the LLM era. DistilBERT reduced BERT's size by 40% while retaining 97% of its language-understanding capability and being 60% faster, using a triple loss over language modeling, distillation, and cosine distance, motivated by "on-the-edge and/or under constrained computational training or inference budgets" [18]. MobileBERT established the teacher-transfer argument for tiny models, reporting it is "4.3× smaller and 5.5× faster than BERT_BASE" at 25.3M parameters and that teacher transfer is "much better than directly training MobileBERT from scratch" [19].

## Quantization: from 8-bit baselines to 2-bit regimes

The foundational activation-outlier result is LLM.int8(), which showed that at 6.7B scale roughly 150,000 outliers occur per sequence but "are concentrated in only 6 feature dimensions across the entire transformer," about 0.1% of input features; zeroing those dimensions degraded validation perplexity by 600-1000% versus about 0.1% for random features [20]. It cuts inference memory roughly in half by combining vector-wise quantization with a mixed-precision decomposition keeping more than 99.9% of values in 8-bit [20]. SmoothQuant migrates quantization difficulty from activations to weights with a mathematically equivalent transformation, reporting up to 1.56x speedup and 2x memory reduction with negligible accuracy loss and enabling a 530B model to be served within a single node [6]. Its authors also criticize the LLM.int8() decomposition as hard to implement efficiently on hardware accelerators — an early sign that algorithmic elegance and kernel efficiency diverge [6].

Weight-only methods attacked the problem from the weight side. GPTQ, a one-shot method based on approximate second-order information, quantized 175B-parameter models in about four GPU hours to 3-4 bits per weight, reporting "around 3.25x" speedup on A100 and "4.5x" on A6000 [2]. AWQ argued that salience should be read from the activation distribution rather than weights, that protecting just 1% of salient channels via an equivalent scaling transform suffices, and that avoiding mixed precision prevents calibration overfitting; it reported more than 3x speedup over the Hugging Face FP16 implementation and a roughly 4x lower memory footprint, enabling a 13B model at an interactive 30 tokens/second on a laptop RTX 4070 GPU with only 8GB of memory [7]. The two define a live disagreement: AWQ's authors state that "the reconstruction process of GPTQ leads to an over-fitting issue to the calibration set" [7].

Extreme low-bit work has followed, alongside attempts to make comparisons trustworthy. VPTQ uses second-order optimization with vector quantization and lookup tables down to 2 bits, reducing perplexity by 0.01-0.34 on LLaMA-2 but 4.41-7.34 on LLaMA-3 relative to prior state of the art, while using only 10.4-18.6% of the baseline quantization algorithm's execution time for a 1.6-1.8x throughput gain [21]. LLMC addresses the opposite problem — that "quantization configurations vary from each other and cannot be fairly compared" — with a plug-and-play toolkit spanning integer to floating-point, LLM to vision-language models, and fixed to mixed precision [22]. On edge hardware, Vec-LUT notes deployment pressure has pushed quantization "from 8-bit to 4-bit, 2-bit, and now 1.58-bit," identifies scalar lookup tables as a bandwidth bottleneck, and replaces them with a vector lookup performing a single 1→N lookup per index, reporting up to 4.2x improvement over state-of-the-art baselines across 5 edge devices and 3 LLMs [23].

The recurring caveat is that memory savings do not automatically become latency savings: the compression survey states weight-only quantization "inevitably introduces additional computational overhead" because weights must be dequantized before multiplication and therefore "cannot enjoy the accelerated low-bit operation supported by specific hardware" [1], and GPTQ's authors confirm the gap concretely [2].

## Pruning, sparsity and the weight-space view

Pruning offers an orthogonal axis, with the striking property that large models tolerate more one-shot damage than small ones. SparseGPT showed GPT-family models can be pruned "to at least 50% sparsity in one-shot, without any retraining, at minimal loss of accuracy," reaching 60% unstructured sparsity on OPT-175B and BLOOM-176B in under 4.5 hours, generalizing to semi-structured 2:4 and 4:8 patterns and composing with quantization [24]. The taxonomy clarifies the granularity tradeoff: unstructured pruning removes individual parameters, structured pruning "removes entire components like neurons, attention heads, or layers," and semi-structured approaches follow fixed patterns, with hardware friendliness improving as structure increases [1].

Post-training pruning has since moved toward unifying fast local scoring with stable global coordination. UniPruning combines "fast layer-wise scoring and a lightweight global controller to allocate a single sparsity budget," supports unstructured and N:M masks in one framework, and generates masks for arbitrary sparsity levels in one shot after a brief calibration, motivated by the failure mode that "local metric approaches prune layer by layer but often collapse under high sparsity" [25]. A striking recent result blurs pruning and parameter-efficient fine-tuning: Super-Tuning reuses pruning saliency signals to choose where a model should adapt, noting low-score supports under magnitude and Wanda-style orderings "can be effective" — the weights a pruner would discard can be the best places to attach an adapter. With matched trainable-parameter budgets, its Supra variant averaged 62.23 accuracy versus 61.07 for LoRA and 12.64 for the base model on Llama-3.2-1B (full fine-tuning reached 65.70), though results are single-seed and schedule-selected [26].

## SLM training: overtraining, data curation and distillation

The dominant recipe is deliberate overtraining beyond compute-optimal token counts, paired with aggressive data curation. TinyLlama trains a 1.1B model on up to 3 trillion tokens, explicitly exploring "the behavior of smaller models when trained with a significantly larger number of tokens than what is suggested by the scaling law," using a SlimPajama/StarCoder mixture at a 7:3 natural-language-to-code ratio over 16 A100-40G GPUs, and reports surpassing OPT-1.3B and Pythia-1.4B [8].

Phi-3 makes data quality the whole thesis. Its 3.8B mini model, trained on 3.3T tokens of "heavily filtered publicly available web data and synthetic data," reaches 69% on MMLU and 8.38 on MT-bench — competitive with Mixtral 8x7B and GPT-3.5 — while quantizing to 4 bits at approximately 1.8GB and running fully offline on an iPhone 14 at more than 12 tokens per second; its authors state that "the innovation lies entirely in our dataset for training" [5]. Qwen2 extends overtraining to genuinely tiny scales, describing its 0.5B and 1.5B dense models as "designed for easy deployment on portable devices such as smartphones, earphones, and smart glasses"; its ablation is instructive because the 0.5B model used a 12T-token set while larger models used 7T, since the larger dataset "did not show a significant performance improvement" [27]. SmolLM2 overtrained a 1.7B model on ~11T tokens via a multi-stage process mixing web text with specialized math, code, and instruction data, introducing FineMath, Stack-Edu, and SmolTalk, and arguing that "data curation has an especially outsized influence for smaller models, as their limited capacity must be carefully optimized for learning core knowledge and fundamental capabilities rather than memorizing incidental facts"; training cost was near 1e23 FLOPs, or roughly $250,000 of GPU compute [9].

Distillation is the third path, and Gemma 2 commits to it most strongly: its 2B and 9B models are trained with knowledge distillation rather than next-token prediction, minimizing negative log-likelihood between teacher and student probabilities to simulate training beyond the available tokens [10]. It reports training these models on more than 50x the compute-optimal token quantity, and its ablation shows distillation from a 7B teacher beating from-scratch training at matched budgets (perplexity 21/17/15 distilled versus 23/19/17 from scratch at 200M/400M/1B tokens) [10]. This is the clearest counterweight to pure overtraining: at very large token budgets, adding a distillation objective can beat adding more raw tokens. The rationale-based line adds another lever for task models, with Distilling Step-by-Step reporting a finetuned 770M T5 outperforming few-shot prompted 540B PaLM using 80% of available data, with up to over 85% fewer training examples [28]. A study of 0.5B reasoning models frames the remaining gap candidly, noting "the limited capacity of 0.5 billion parameter models poses challenges in handling complex tasks such as mathematical reasoning and code generation" while surveying SFT, distillation, and reinforcement-learning hybrids [29].

## Attention, KV cache and alternative sequence architectures

Because autoregressive decoding is memory-bandwidth bound — every step moves full parameters from high-bandwidth memory to the accelerator cache [30] — kernel and architecture work targets memory traffic rather than arithmetic. FlashAttention reframes attention as an IO problem, using tiling to cut HBM reads and writes, reporting up to 9x fewer HBM accesses, roughly 3x speedup on GPT-2, and memory linear in sequence length versus standard attention's quadratic footprint [31]. The KV-specific line began with shared key/value heads: multi-query attention shares keys and values across heads to reduce "the memory bandwidth requirements of incremental decoding," motivated by the observation that "the memory-bandwidth cost of repeatedly loading the large 'keys' and 'values' tensors" dominates incremental inference, and reports per-token cost falling from about 46 microseconds to about 3.8 microseconds in its analysis [32].

PagedAttention and vLLM moved the insight into systems. Treating the KV cache like operating-system virtual memory, PagedAttention stores continuous keys and values in non-contiguous fixed-size blocks, achieving near-zero KV memory waste and flexible sharing within and across requests, and improving throughput "by 2-4x with the same level of latency compared to the state-of-the-art systems, such as FasterTransformer and Orca" [3]. H2O attacks the cache statistically instead: observing that "a small portion of tokens contributes most of the value when computing attention scores," it evicts the rest with a dynamic submodular policy, reducing KV memory by up to 5x without accuracy degradation and improving throughput by up to 29x over DeepSpeed Zero-Inference and Hugging Face Accelerate [33].

Recent work pushes compression much harder while trying to preserve semantics. RocketKV uses coarse-grain permanent eviction followed by fine-grain top-k sparse attention, reporting compression up to 400x, end-to-end speedup up to 3.7x, and peak decode-phase memory reduction up to 32.6% on an A100 [11]. ChunkKV changes the compression unit from individual tokens to semantic chunks with layer-wise index reuse, reporting up to 10% improvement under aggressive compression ratios on LongBench and Needle-In-A-Haystack [34]. CompressKV identifies "Semantic Retrieval Heads" capturing both prompt endpoints and mid-context evidence and allocates cache budget across layers by offline eviction-error estimates, claiming over 97% of full-cache performance using only 3% of the KV cache on LongBench QA [35]. On the systems side, SparseEngine packages 15 KV-eviction methods with cross-request state management and controllable prefix-cache pruning, reporting over 10x higher throughput with KV eviction, over 2.5x faster decoding at matched concurrency than vLLM, and over 2x end-to-end speedup on agent benchmarks [13].

A parallel bet avoids the growing cache entirely. Mamba makes state-space parameters input-dependent so the model can "selectively propagate or forget information along the sequence length dimension," requiring a hardware-aware recurrent-mode algorithm; it reports 5x higher throughput than Transformers, linear scaling in sequence length, and constant time per autoregressive step because it needs no cache of previous elements [36]. Jamba hedges by interleaving Transformer and Mamba blocks with mixture-of-experts, aiming for "an 8x smaller KV cache compared to a vanilla Transformer" while keeping strong results up to 256K tokens of context [12].

## Speculative decoding and serving algorithms

Speculative decoding exploits the gap between memory-bound decoding and available compute. The foundational formulation drafts several tokens with a smaller approximation model and verifies them in parallel with the target model using speculative sampling, so outputs are unchanged; it requires "no retraining or architecture changes" and reports 2x-3x acceleration on T5-XXL with identical outputs [4]. Its stated limit matters: latency improves through increased concurrency "at the cost of an increased number of arithmetic operations," so the method does not help when additional compute is unavailable [4].

Later work removes the separate draft model. Medusa adds extra decoding heads that predict multiple subsequent tokens, verified simultaneously with a tree-based attention mechanism; Medusa-1 trains heads on a frozen backbone for what the authors call "lossless inference acceleration" and reports over 2.2x speedup without compromising quality, rising to 2.3-2.8x when the backbone is trained jointly [30]. A survey of the area summarizes the paradigm as drafting future tokens and verifying them in parallel so that "multiple tokens per step" are decoded [37]. Together with paged serving [3], these raise throughput without changing the model distribution, which is why they compose well with quantization and pruning.

## On-device deployment, hardware and efficiency measurement

Deployment reality is messier than headline tok/s figures, and the most useful recent contributions are measurement studies. A controlled llama.cpp study on an iPhone 15 Pro found a CPU-only configuration with two threads at F16 precision reached 17 tokens/second versus 12.8 with GPU acceleration, and that for models such as Qwen2-0.5B and LLaMA-3.2-1B multi-threaded CPU execution beat GPU by 1.31x and 1.33x respectively, while GPUs regained the lead above roughly 1.5B parameters; F16→Q4 quantization yielded 1.5x-2.5x speedup, most prominently at higher CPU thread counts, and matrix multiplication dominated execution time (87.6% in prefill, 76.2% in decode) [14]. The authors caution that "fully explaining the observed CPU advantage remains difficult due to limited access to low-level profiling tools on iOS" [14].

Independent quantization evaluation reinforces that more compression is not uniformly better. A unified empirical study covers 3-8 bit K-quant and legacy llama.cpp formats on Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct, evaluating downstream task performance alongside model size, compression, and CPU prefill/decoding throughput [38]. A community cross-platform dataset adds a hardware-dependent wrinkle: across GGUF K-quants on Llama 3.2 3B and Qwen 2.5 1.5B, Q2_K was roughly 99% faster than Q6_K on a Pixel 6a "despite having less than half the bits per weight," yet the same configuration suffered a -48% throughput cliff beyond about 512 tokens, showing aggressive quantization can interact badly with KV-cache growth [39].

Energy measurement has become its own subfield. The ML.ENERGY Benchmark reports energy measurements of 40 widely used model architectures across 6 tasks, argues energy should be attributed at the granularity of a complete generation response rather than per token, and claims automated optimization recommendations "can lead to significant (sometimes more than 40%) energy savings without changing what is being computed by the model" [15]. Its data API exposes steady-state energy per token and per request and drops runs whose steady state is shorter than 20 seconds or whose batch utilization falls below 85% of the configured maximum — a reminder that energy numbers are extremely sensitive to serving configuration [40]. MobileAIBench complements this by measuring latency and resource consumption on real devices via an iOS app, attending to "quantization's impact on various task performances, including LLM tasks, LMM tasks, and, critically, trust and safety" [41].

Two further directions connect SLMs to deployment economics. SlimLM tested model sizes from 125M to 7B on a Samsung Galaxy S24 to find optimal tradeoffs between size, context length, and inference time for document assistance, aiming to reduce server costs and enhance privacy [42]. Minions instead makes the split explicit: a small on-device model communicates with a frontier cloud model, where a naive protocol cuts remote cost 30.4x but recovers only 87% of frontier performance, while a decomposed protocol executing subtasks locally in parallel reduces costs 5.7x on average while recovering 97.9% of remote-model performance [43]. HyperVL identifies the ViT encoder as "a critical bottleneck" for on-device multimodal systems and reports a 6.8x reduction in peak memory overhead with robustness to W4A16 quantization [44].

## Trends and open problems

Four trends stand out. First, efficiency is pursued simultaneously at the weight, activation, state, and system layers, and the layers compose: quantization and pruning reduce what must be loaded [2][24], attention and cache redesigns reduce what must be stored [31][11], speculative decoding and paged serving reduce idle hardware time [4][3], and recent engines package them together [13]. Second, the binding constraint has shifted from parameters to the KV cache and long-context state, which is why 2025-2026 work reports compression ratios in the hundreds and throughput multiples rather than modest bit-width reductions [11][13][35]. Third, "small" is now achieved by deliberate overtraining and data curation rather than architecture alone, with Phi-3 making synthetic data the central claim [5] and SmolLM2 making curation the explicit lever for models under ~3B [9]. Fourth, evaluation has become a first-class research object: LLMC exists because configurations "cannot be fairly compared" [22], the ML.ENERGY Benchmark exists because energy accounting was inconsistent [15], and on-device studies exist because vendor throughput claims rarely specify threads, precision, or context length [14][38].

Several results remain disputed or conditional. GPTQ, SmoothQuant, and AWQ report 3-4.5x speedups, yet GPTQ's own text notes no speedup for the multiplications due to missing mixed-precision hardware support, and AWQ accuses GPTQ's reconstruction of overfitting the calibration set — so the accuracy-versus-speed frontier is method- and hardware-dependent rather than settled [2][6][7]. Speculative decoding is explicitly not free: it trades arithmetic for latency and is unhelpful when compute is scarce [4]. Aggressive low-bit quantization can reverse performance on particular devices and context lengths, as the Q2_K throughput cliff shows [39]. Pruning's most counterintuitive recent result — that low-saliency weights can be the best places to attach adapters — rests on single-seed comparisons [26].

Open problems follow from these tensions. Methods rarely report whether memory savings convert into wall-clock latency on the specific target hardware, and the mixed-precision support gap called out years ago is still visible in the llama.cpp lineage [1][2][38]. Distillation and overtraining compete directly at large token budgets, and no source here establishes when each wins: Gemma 2's ablation favors distillation at matched tokens [10], while Phi-3 and SmolLM2 attribute gains primarily to data rather than a teacher [5][9]. KV-compression claims rely on task suites such as LongBench and Needle-in-a-Haystack [34][35], which may not capture agentic multi-turn behavior; the one engine reviewed here explicitly targets agent workloads and cross-request cache reuse, suggesting evaluation practice is starting to catch up [13]. Energy per token is measurable but configuration-sensitive [15][40], and cost-per-million-tokens — the metric most decision-makers care about — was not found in any retrieved primary source, leaving a gap between research metrics and procurement metrics. Finally, hybrid and SSM architectures promise cache-light inference [36][12], but the sources reviewed report architectural efficiency rather than head-to-head instruction-following quality against well-tuned Transformers at equal scale. Efficiency gains are real and large, but conditional on hardware, serving configuration, and task.
